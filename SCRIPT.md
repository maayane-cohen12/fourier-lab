# The Fourier Transform — Explainer Script

**Goal:** The viewer understands that any sound is a sum of simple sine waves, and that the Fourier transform finds which frequencies are in it and how strong each one is.

## Narration

Audio for each row is generated by `narrate.py` into `public/audio/sceneN.wav`.

| # | Scene | Narration |
|---|-------|-----------|
| 1 | Intro | Every sound you hear is hiding a secret. In the next two minutes, let's see how a computer takes a sound apart. |
| 2 | MessyWave | This wiggly line is what sound looks like to a computer. It looks like chaos, but there is a hidden pattern inside. |
| 3 | WhatIsASine | Start with the simplest sound there is: a sine wave. How fast it wiggles is its frequency, which we hear as pitch. How tall it is, its amplitude, is how loud it is. |
| 4 | SumOfSines | Here are three simple, smooth waves: a low note, a middle note, and a high note. Add them together, point by point, and you get exactly that messy wave. |
| 5 | SplitBack | The Fourier transform does the reverse. It takes the messy wave and finds the simple waves hidden inside it. |
| 6 | HowItFinds | How does it find them? It tries one frequency at a time and compares it with the sound. When they match, they line up and the score is high. When they don't, they cancel out and the score is close to zero. |
| 7 | Spectrum | Each simple wave becomes one bar. Where the bar sits tells you the frequency, and how tall it is tells you how strong it is. |
| 8 | Filter | Now remove one bar, and the sound changes. That is how filters work: any sound is just a sum of sine waves. |
| 9 | EverydayUses | You use this every day: music apps compress songs, noise-cancelling headphones remove hum, and Shazam recognizes a song from its frequencies. |
| 10 | Recap | So remember: any sound is a sum of sine waves, and the Fourier transform tells us which ones, and how strong each one is. |

## Visual notes

1. **Intro.** A dark screen with a speaker or sound icon pulsing; a short burst of waveform flickers across, then the title "The Fourier Transform" appears.
2. **MessyWave.** A white, glowing complex wave scrolls across the full screen with yellow sample dots, under the title and subtitle. It slows to a stop, shrinks into the Sum row at the bottom, and dims to a faint outline labeled "Sum".
3. **WhatIsASine.** Starts on MessyWave's last frame (faint Sum outline). One clean sine wave appears; its wiggles speed up and slow down under a "frequency → pitch" label, then it grows and shrinks under an "amplitude → loudness" label. It clears away and the scene ends on that same last frame, so SumOfSines continues seamlessly.
4. **SumOfSines.** Three sine rows (220 Hz blue, 440 Hz orange, 660 Hz pink) draw in above the Sum row, with "+" and "=" signs. A dashed scan line sweeps left to right, stacking each row's value into the Sum row and tracing the white sum over the faint outline; caption: "Three simple waves → one messy sound".
5. **SplitBack.** The reverse of scene 4: the three colored sine waves are peeled out of the white messy wave, until all three are separated again.
6. **HowItFinds.** A test sine wave slides over the messy wave while a score meter updates: at a matching frequency the peaks line up and the meter jumps high; at a non-matching one the shaded overlap cancels out and the meter sits near zero.
7. **Spectrum.** Each sine wave collapses into a colored bar on a frequency axis (low → high, left → right); bar height matches the wave's amplitude, with "frequency" and "strength" labels pointing to position and height.
8. **Filter.** One bar (e.g. the high note) fades out; the reconstructed waveform above visibly smooths as that component disappears.
9. **EverydayUses.** Three quick vignettes, each with a small spectrum: a music file shrinking as faint bars are dropped, headphones with a low hum bar being removed, and a phone matching a spectrum fingerprint to a song.
10. **Recap.** The messy wave splits into its three sine waves and their bars side by side, ending on the caption "Any sound = a sum of sine waves."
